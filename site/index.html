Standard methods waste value in the same three ways

Unused capacity

Budget is spread evenly where only some parts need it, so the parts that need it get too little.

Unneeded messages

Agents and people resend the whole history when only one missing piece is needed.

Unvalued futures

Each step is chosen for now, not for what it does to the next steps and the plan as a whole.

These patterns showed up in every domain tested; see paper §1–§2.

How it works

At every step the seed looks at the options the domain already has, plus its own past and planned actions, actions focused on one part, and actions on parts that drive each other. It values each one in the unit's own possible futures, as a whole plan, and takes it only if it is at least as good as the standard action in those same futures.

Three habits follow: see the whole and value each option in its own future; act only where it is needed; say only what is missing.

What is claimed, and what is not

Claimed: one sealed selection discipline across domains, replicated on fresh cells. The selector is shared; the candidate actions are written per domain. A sealed ablation found the bare structure alone no better than plain rollout; with its operators bound, the structure beats plain rollout in three hosts (CONF5, CONF6).

Not claimed: the best method in every field, production-scale savings, or anything beyond the sealed runs. In REAL3 the comparisons with stronger methods are unequal in information or architecture; see paper §7.

Each part of the seed has prior art (rollout, scenario MPC, plan reuse, early-stopping self-consistency, receiver-relative messaging, answering on sufficient evidence): prior-art search, update of 26 Nov. Not found: one operator language bound across hosts this unrelated.

Replicated on fresh cells, and what the ablation showed

A second sealed run (STRUCT1, cells 301–308) repeated the full seed with an earlier flaw fixed and found the same: 45–57 % fewer tokens in AI agents with higher accuracy, 43 % in AI–human replies, +1.3 % in inventory and satellite downlink. The same run switched off every candidate written for a specific domain: the general structure alone then did no better than plain rollout in any row (pre-registered level: not met). The gains come from the domain-written candidates the seed selects among (paper §5.4).

Earlier sealed evidence: REAL1 and REAL1b with an earlier seed (REAL1, REAL1b); a controlled simulated world (SEAL2, SEAL1, 13 of 15 rows); blind planning, 256 of 256 tasks exact across six models (report). Limits and every disclosed fix: paper §8.

Pre-registration, fingerprints of every file, fresh cells, paired comparisons with intervals, every cell rerunnable, every fix disclosed. The method above, as an open protocol: a specification, a one-file node and a public log anyone can join. SRC-X.5: the open record behind every result

Every sealed test on this page is a record in SRC-X.5, an open protocol for sealed claims. A record states what was predicted and by which criteria, which files were fixed and when, when the test ran, what came out and who replicated it. Its id is the SHA-256 of its canonical form, so any copy is verified independently of the host or network it came from. It carries no domain content and no personal data, so any field can use it.

The language behind it

RSOS is a functional design language: symbols are components, operators act on them, and the order of objects in a scene is the program. The seed tested above is one part of it. Colours in the language carry meaning:

turquoise: proven in layer 2, the RSOS model world blue: proven in layer 1, the real world cyan: proven in both layers green: done yellow: started grey: not started; in a design, risky but contained black: in a design, only destructive violet: the family of shades, finite distinguishable units in any host
